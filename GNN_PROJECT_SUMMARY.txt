GNN PROJECT SUMMARY
===================

Project title
-------------
Counterfactual-Induced Patient Migration for Graph-Based Cardiovascular Disease Risk Prediction

Project name
------------
Dynamic Patient Similarity Network (DPSN) for cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk prediction.

1. Problem statement
--------------------
Traditional CVD prediction models estimate whether a patient is at risk, but they do not explain how a patient's relationships with similar patients change after a possible health intervention. A normal counterfactual asks questions such as: "What would happen to the predicted risk if blood pressure, cholesterol, BMI, or lifestyle factors improved?"

The missing graph-based question was: "After those features improve, does the patient move to a different group of similar patients, especially a group with lower CVD risk?"

The project therefore addressed the following problem:

- Build a patient similarity graph in which each patient is a node.
- Train a Graph Neural Network to predict CVD risk from patient features and graph relationships.
- Generate realistic, constrained counterfactual versions of high-risk patients.
- Recompute each counterfactual patient's graph neighborhood instead of reusing the original neighbors.
- Measure the resulting patient migration and determine whether it provides information beyond a risk-score change alone.

The central research contribution is Counterfactual Patient Migration (CPM): measuring the structural movement of a patient through a clinical similarity network after a hypothetical intervention.

2. How the problem was solved
-----------------------------

Step 1: Data preparation
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
The project used a cleaned CVD dataset containing 1,000 patients, clinical and lifestyle features, and a binary CVD target. The feature set included age, blood pressure, cholesterol, heart rate, BMI, physical activity, ECG score, glucose, triglycerides, stress index, and binary risk factors such as smoking and diabetes. Patient identifiers were excluded from model features. The data pipeline checked missing values, duplicates, data types, class balance, feature ranges, and outliers.

Step 2: Baseline model comparison
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Classical machine-learning models were trained as reference baselines, including Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and SVM. These models provided a comparison point for the graph-based models.

Step 3: Patient similarity graph construction
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Patients were represented as graph nodes. Edges connected clinically similar patients using several graph construction strategies, including Euclidean KNN, cosine similarity, clinical similarity, and a dynamic patient similarity graph. The clinical graph used domain-weighted feature distances so that medically important variables could contribute appropriately to similarity.

Step 4: Graph Neural Network risk prediction
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT models were trained on the patient graphs. GraphSAGE performance was evaluated across graph types and the graph configuration was selected using validation performance. The best recorded GNN configuration was GraphSAGE with the clinical-similarity graph.

Step 5: High-risk cohort selection
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
A high-risk cohort was selected using the trained model's predicted probabilities. The development population contained 39 high-risk patients for counterfactual analysis.

Step 6: Constrained counterfactual generation
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
For every selected high-risk patient, a healthier hypothetical patient was generated by changing permitted modifiable variables within plausible limits while preserving non-modifiable patient characteristics. The objective was to reduce predicted CVD risk below the target threshold with clinically constrained feature changes.

Step 7: Graph position rebuild
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
For each successful counterfactual, similarity was recomputed against the full patient population. The new K-nearest-neighbor set was calculated from the counterfactual feature vector. This ensured that migration was measured from a genuine graph rebuild rather than by assuming that the original neighborhood remained valid.

Step 8: Migration measurement
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
The project measured:

- Neighbor turnover: the proportion of original neighbors replaced after intervention.
- Neighborhood risk shift: the change in CVD prevalence or risk in the patient's neighborhood.
- Embedding displacement: movement in the learned GraphSAGE representation space.
- CPM score: a weighted summary of topology and representation changes.

The approved CPM formula was:

    CPM = 0.40 * T + 0.40 * R + 0.20 * D_norm

where:

- T is normalized neighbor turnover.
- R is normalized neighborhood risk shift.
- D_norm is normalized GraphSAGE embedding displacement.

Feature-change effort was reported separately so that the CPM score did not hide the amount of intervention required.

Step 9: Three-way comparison
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
The same cohort was evaluated under three conditions:

1. Normal counterfactual: risk-score change only.
2. Fixed-graph counterfactual: risk-score change while keeping the original neighbors fixed.
3. Topology-aware counterfactual: risk-score change together with recomputed graph migration.

This comparison tested whether graph topology adds information that is missed by a conventional counterfactual explanation.

Step 10: Held-out validation
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
The migration analysis was repeated on a held-out test split from the same dataset. The test split contained only 10 high-risk patients, below the project's preferred minimum of 15. Bootstrap confidence intervals were therefore used, and the test result was treated as directional rather than fully powered confirmation.

3. Final output and results
---------------------------

Dataset and synthetic validation
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
- Total dataset: 1,000 patients.
- Development population: 800 patients.
- Held-out test population: 200 patients.
- Synthetic population: 2,000 patients generated with CTGAN.
- Synthetic CVD class ratio: 0.1931 compared with 0.1933 in the real reference data.
- Synthetic-data validation passed the recorded KS-distribution checks, correlation check, class-balance check, and clinical-range plausibility checks.

Risk prediction results
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Best recorded GNN configuration:

- Model: GraphSAGE.
- Graph: Clinical Similarity.
- Accuracy: 0.9350.
- Precision: 0.7708.
- Recall: 0.9487.
- F1 score: 0.8506.
- ROC-AUC: 0.9892.
- PR-AUC: 0.9632.
- MCC: 0.8167.

Best classical baseline:

- Model: LightGBM.
- Test F1 score: 0.8941.
- Test ROC-AUC: 0.9893.

The separate DPSN evidence report recorded a DPSN F1 score of 0.8132 and a ROC-AUC of 0.988, with statistically significant differences in 17 of 24 reported pairwise comparisons. These values describe the broader DPSN evaluation, while the GraphSAGE values above describe the best GNN configuration in the GNN metrics artifact.

Counterfactual results
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
- High-risk patients analyzed: 39.
- Successful counterfactuals: 39.
- Counterfactual success rate: 100 percent.
- Mean risk reduction: 0.7923.
- Mean number of changed features: 8.9.
- Target risk threshold: 0.5.

The most frequently changed variables included blood pressure, cholesterol, heart rate, physical activity, triglycerides, stress index, glucose, BMI, smoking, and diabetes status. ECG score was unchanged in the recorded counterfactual outputs.

Graph migration results
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
- Patients with rebuilt graph positions: 39.
- Mean neighbor turnover: 0.9831.
- Mean neighborhood prevalence shift: 0.6156.
- Mean GraphSAGE embedding displacement: 10.3649.
- Mean normalized neighborhood risk shift: 0.6180.
- Mean normalized embedding displacement: 0.6109.
- Mean CPM score: 0.7626.
- CPM standard deviation: 0.1693.
- CPM range: 0.2705 to 0.9440.

These results show that the healthier counterfactual patients were not simply assigned lower risk scores. Their graph neighborhoods changed substantially, indicating movement toward a different region of the patient similarity network.

Three-way comparison results
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Development cohort results for 39 patients:

- Normal counterfactual mean risk reduction: 0.7923.
- Fixed-graph counterfactual mean risk reduction: 0.0000.
- Topology-aware counterfactual mean risk reduction: 0.0009.
- Topology-aware advantage was positive for 71.79 percent of patients.
- Spearman sensitivity correlation: 0.8101.
- Reported Wilcoxon tests were statistically significant for all three pairwise comparisons, with p-values below 0.001.

Held-out test validation results:

- Validation cohort: 10 high-risk patients.
- Normal counterfactual mean risk reduction: 0.7421.
- Fixed-graph counterfactual mean risk reduction: 0.0000.
- Topology-aware counterfactual mean risk reduction: 0.00061.
- Mean neighbor turnover: 0.99156.
- Mean normalized risk shift: 0.65845.
- Direction of migration effects: consistent with the development population.
- Limitation: n=10 is below the preferred minimum, so this result is directional only.

5. Final conclusion
-------------------
The project solved the problem by combining clinical patient-similarity graphs, GraphSAGE risk prediction, constrained counterfactual generation, and full graph-neighborhood recomputation. The final analysis demonstrates that a counterfactual intervention can produce substantial patient migration in graph space, not merely a lower predicted CVD risk.

The main conclusion is that graph-aware counterfactual analysis provides an additional structural explanation: it describes which peer group a patient moves toward after a hypothetical intervention and how much that neighborhood changes. The result supports CPM as a useful research metric for explaining topology-aware changes in graph-based clinical risk prediction, while the small held-out high-risk test cohort should be expanded in future work for stronger statistical validation.
